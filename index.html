Thoughts

The forgotten country


  I understand the game is about the absence of memories of the island, but I like thinking about how many times Wish Craft went wrong, if there were safety measures, guilds, the reason someone might have wanted to erase the entire country in the first place... So so much about the island that goes, I suppose intentionally, unsaid.

  Notably---is Siffrin's understanding of Wishcraft unique? The King uses it, also, but are two islanders using Wishcraft enough to assume Wish Craft was a prominent part of daily life on the island?

  We know that Siffrin's Wish was amplified by the Vaugardians' hope for the group to succeed in defeating the King, so it takes a significant amount of belief for a ritual to succeed. At the same time that it's reasonable to theorize that there were strict rules in place for how Wish Craft could be used, it might be simpler to assume that the chances of a ritual succeeding were so slim that every non-lethal Wish coming true was just brushed aside as an outlier until it was too late. Certainly wouldn't be a new concept.

  Grief about not being able to return to something or someone (whether that person be those close to yourself or just the person you used to be) is a central theme in ISAT. The island's primary religion is the Universe, which bears stark contrast to Change: one is content to follow the Universe's lead, while the other encourages constant self-driven change (to self, personality, aspirations). It's interesting to think about where the two religions overlap and where they diverge, but it's not talked about in much detail in-game. This is probably because it takes a majority of the game to reach an understanding of the forgotten country's disapperance at all. You can only cover so many bases in one game.

Sadnesses


  Another thing I think is a little under-explored in the game is the concept of Sadnesses, the main enemies in ISAT.

  In the game, Sadnesses are described by Odile to be hostile entities that are "all that remains" of the people defeatd by the King. They are embodiments of intense negative emotion, have existed throughout history, and they proliferate during times of strife like war or resource scarcity. "Defeating them is all [that can be done]."

  The Sadnesses are simultaneously described as the people themselves (albeit their remnants) rather than the byproducts of their emotions, while the people they used to be remained frozen in time. They can regenerate after they are defeated, and killing them doesn't affect those who are frozen, so there isn't any reason they can't be killed without remorse.

  Then---why introduce Sadnesses that protect each other, and have the party members comment on feeling guilty about killing them? Given Bonnie's curiosity about everything else, it struck me as odd that it's never addressed how Bonnie's sister would exist a Sadness. It's not enough to call a plothole, but it might have been interesting to delve a little more into the party's feelings about having to fight them.



(Not to mention Mal du Pays, who is immune to all attacks, chooses to attack only Siffrin, is still at large, and throws a wrench into what actually defines a Sadness, but I digress)
